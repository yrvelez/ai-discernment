## Summary

A US online-panel survey experiment (raw N = 2,257; analysis N = 2,030) randomised participants to eleven interventions or a no-intervention control (n = 181) and measured accuracy at classifying real versus AI-generated items. The study was not pre-registered, so every test is post hoc; no multiple-testing correction was applied. Pooled across arms, total accuracy was not distinguishable from control (+0.010, SE 0.007, p = 0.132, two-sided). Automated Flagging (+0.044, p = 0.004) and AI Literacy Guide (+0.047, p = 0.043) scored above control on total accuracy; Mindfulness scored below (-0.039, p = 0.032). For AI-generated items the pooled effect was +0.038 (p = 0.0004, two-sided), with the Breathing Exercise, AI Accuracy Nudge and AI Literacy Guide arms above control. For real items, only AI Literacy Infographic 2 differed from control, scoring lower (-0.061, p = 0.019). With 33 post hoc tests, several near p = 0.05, findings are hypothesis-generating.

## Related findings (minimal literature note)

The retrieved literature offers no direct experimental evidence on H1–H3: none of the works tests an intervention intended to change human accuracy at discriminating AI-generated from real items, whether overall or by item type. The closest works address adjacent constructs—reviews of misinformation belief and its resistance to correction (Ecker et al., 2022) and of fake news and disinformation in social media [citation removed: not in retrieved set] examine why false content is believed and how corrections fare, while discussion of ChatGPT in higher education notes that AI-generated prose can be impressively fluent and thus hard to identify (Rudolph et al., 2023). These works motivate the plausibility that discernment is difficult and that interventions may matter, but they do not report effect sizes or item-type-specific accuracy changes of the kind the hypotheses require.

The remainder of the retrieved set—covering LLM training curricula, radiology AI, behavioral policy framing, LLM security, retrieval-augmented generation, IoT finance, and generative AI in education—is off-topic for these hypotheses. Overall, the list is thin for the present research question, and the study's hypotheses appear largely untested by the retrieved prior work.

## Limitations

Thirty-three arm-level tests were run without multiple-testing correction, so some significant results are likely false positives; the AI Literacy Guide effects (p = 0.043 and p = 0.049) sit at the 0.05 threshold and should be read cautiously. Several arms are small (Inoculation n = 76-78; AI Literacy Infographic 2 n = 79-80), so their estimates are imprecise with wide confidence intervals. Missing outcome data slightly reduced the H2 and H3 samples (2,023 and 2,025 of 2,030 eligible). The pooled estimates combine heterogeneous arms that share one control group (H1 I^2 = 0.445), and the H3 pooled estimate was not available in the provided tables. Participants came from a US online panel, limiting generalisation. The retrieved literature contains no direct prior experimental tests of these interventions, so results cannot be benchmarked against prior effect sizes.
