The File Drawer

Improving AI Discernment

Columbia University
IRB Protocol: AAAU9484

Executive Summary

As generative AI makes synthetic media increasingly indistinguishable from authentic content, the ability to detect AI-generated images and videos has become a critical media literacy skill. Deepfakes of political figures, AI-generated protest photos, and synthetic news content pose serious risks to democratic discourse and public trust.

This study tested whether interventions proven effective against misinformation could be adapted to improve AI discernment. We evaluated 12 treatments—including inoculation, accuracy nudges, mindfulness exercises, and AI literacy training—drawn from the misinformation literature. The results reveal a sobering challenge: most interventions that work for misinformation do not transfer to AI detection.

The Translation Problem

Detecting AI-generated content requires different cognitive processes than evaluating misinformation. While misinformation interventions target motivated reasoning and source credibility assessment, AI detection demands perceptual attention to visual artifacts—a fundamentally different skill that existing interventions fail to address.

What Works: Direct Training

T10 (AI Literacy Guide) improved accuracy by 4.8pp (p=0.036) and T3 (Automated Flagging) by 4.4pp (p=0.004). Both provide concrete, specific information about AI artifacts rather than general critical thinking prompts.

Backfire Effect

T6 (Mindfulness) reduced accuracy by 4.0pp (p=0.027). Slowing down and reflecting—effective for misinformation—may actually impair the rapid perceptual judgments needed for AI detection.

Mostly Null Results

Eight interventions showed no significant effects, including inoculation (prebunking), accuracy nudges, and breathing exercises. The misinformation playbook does not straightforwardly apply to AI discernment.

The Stimulus: FutureFeed Platform

Participants viewed content on a custom social media platform called "FutureFeed" — a simulated feed mixing real news with AI-generated content. Each post included Like and Share buttons to simulate authentic social media engagement.

Outcome Measures

Discernment Task

Participants scrolled through the FutureFeed social media platform and viewed 24 pieces of content (12 real, 12 AI-generated). For each post, they indicated whether they believed the content was AI-generated or authentic human-created media.

Total Accuracy

Proportion correct across all 24 items (primary outcome)

Deepfake/AI Detection

Correctly identifying deepfakes and AI-fabricated images

Authentic Content Accuracy

Correctly identifying real photos and videos as authentic

Covariates

Models control for: media_trust (confidence in news media), political_interest, pk_score (political knowledge), and ai_scale (prior use of AI tools: ChatGPT, Bing AI, Claude, Character.ai, DALL-E, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion).

Methodology

Design

Participants were recruited via CloudResearch (November 2023) and randomly assigned to one of 12 treatment conditions using adaptive randomization across 5 batches.

Statistical Model: Lin (2013) Estimator

We estimate treatment effects using lm_lin from the estimatr package, which implements Lin's (2013) covariate adjustment for randomized experiments:

lm_lin(outcome ~ treatment,
       covariates = ~ media_trust + political_interest + pk_score + ai_scale,
       weights = 1/pr, clusters = participant_id)

What does this model do?

  • • Demeans covariates: Centers each covariate at its mean (e.g., media_trust_c = media_trust - mean(media_trust))
  • • Includes treatment × covariate interactions: Estimates treatment × media_trust_c, treatment × ai_scale_c, etc.
  • • Inverse probability weights (1/pr): Accounts for adaptive randomization across batches
  • • Cluster-robust standard errors: Clusters by participant to account for within-person correlation across 24 trials

Lin (2013) showed this approach is asymptotically efficient and guarantees precision gains over unadjusted estimators in randomized experiments.

Reference: Lin, W. (2013). "Agnostic Notes on Regression Adjustments to Experimental Data: Reexamining Freedman's Critique." Annals of Applied Statistics, 7(1), 295-318.
